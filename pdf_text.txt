1.1 Background and Context
Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning have made it possible for automated systems
to find patterns in network traffic. Many Artificial Intelligence security systems still need
people to help them and they have a hard time keeping up with new threats.
In recent days,Agentic AI is a way to make cybersecurity systems that can work on
their own. These Agentic AI systems can look at the environment, make decisions and stop
threats all by themselves. They can do this because they have capabilities like reasoning
and planning. This means they can do cybersecurity tasks without any help. Some studies
show that AI can make threat detection more accurate and help with real-time defense
against cyber threats [7], [13].
Agentic AI extends generative AI by improving reasoning, planning, tool use, memory,
and autonomy that helps in enabling goal-directed, long-horizon behavior across real envi-
ronments. In cybersecurity this is really useful for continuously monitoring and responding
quickly to threats. At the same time they expand the attack surface through prompt/-
tool injection, unsafe action execution, memory poisoning, and multi-agent manipulation.
[1][2].
For financial and retirement platforms, it’s found that the agentic methods do comple-
ment the deep anomaly detection and UEBA. They help protect the flows, where the fraud
likelihood is highest, such as rollovers, disbursements, and withdrawals. Increased use of
CNN/LSTM/Transformer ensembles paired with event-driven and SOAR, zero -trust as
governance agentic layers are seen in Architectures.[3]
1.2 Motivation and Importance of the Study
AI is being adopted increasingly by the threat actors themselves to automate reconnais-
sance, social engineering and exploitation. Under high volume of attacks and novelty,
traditional signature or rule pipelines lag. For the finance domain transparency, safe au-
tonomy and governance are essential for adoption; though proactive, autonomous agents
promise anomaly surfacing, reduced analyst load and faster time-to-contain.[3][4]
As indicated from the literature survey autonomous Agentic AI systems improve cov-
erage and response, that use deep ensembles of CNN+LSTM+Transformers, have benefits
over traditional threat detection systems where it can; learn spacial-temporal patterns in
logs/transactions,create UEBA baselines per user/entity, have streaming risk scoring inte-
grated with SOAR playbooks, based on event-driven architectures.[3] These are narrowly
scoped agents that align to NIST CSF 2.0 for better scalability and safety than a monolithic
end-to-end agents. Agents reduce the failure impact on live SOCs.[1][2]
Agentic AI systems that can act on their own have benefits over traditional security
systems. They can watch what is happening on the network, find activities and take action
to stop threats without someone always supervising them. These systems can look at a lot
of security data, find patterns that do not seem right and change how they work as new
threats appear.
Recent studies have shown that Agentic AI systems that can act on their own can
make cybersecurity better by allowing them to think and act independently, continuously
monitor and find threats[8], [9]. This capability makes Agentic AI systems a great option
for the next generation of cybersecurity solutions.

1.3 Problem Definition and Scope
Problem:The development of a cybersecurity framework that is capable of automatically
detecting cyber threats and responding to them using Agentic AI techniques for high-stakes
platforms.
In-scope:(i) defensive agentic workflows (monitoring, detection, triage, response),
(ii) adversarial capabilities unique to agents (reasoning compromise, unsafe tool use, mem-
ory/state poisoning, multi-agent manipulation), (iii) governance and evaluation (CSF 2.0
adaptation, specialized benchmarks).
Out-of-scope:cryptographic payload design and physical robotics beyond cyber.
[1][2][5]
1.4 Research Objectives and Questions
1. Synthesize threats, defenses, and evaluation for agentic AI in cybersecurity. [1][2]
Compare detection/response architectures for finance (UEBA, deep ensembles, trans-
formers, federated learning, agentic orchestration).[3] Map controls to NIST CSF 2.0
and OWASP agentic threat model; identify least-agency guardrails. [5]. Assess RL-
based response automation for latency and adaptability, and its risks. [6]
2.Agentic AI Effectiveness:Analyze how agentic AI systems detect cybersecurity
threats by continuous monitoring network activities and identifying anomalous pat-
terns in complex environments. This objective identifies how agent-based frameworks
improve detection accuracy compared to traditional machine learning approaches [7],
[13].
3.Autonomous Threat Detection:Investigate methods that enable autonomous cy-
bersecurity agents to detect threats in real time without human intervention. Agentic
AI systems analyze and understand system behavior, reason about threats, and auto-
matically initiate defensive actions, improving cybersecurity responsiveness [9], [11].
4.Multimodal Data Integration:Examine how agentic AI models can integrate
multiple data sources such as network traffic logs, system logs, threat intelligence
feeds, and sensor data to enhance anomaly detection performance. Multimodal anal-
ysis can improve the identification of complex and coordinated cyberattack patterns
[15].
5.Adaptive Learning and Decision-Making:Evaluate how agentic AI systems
continuously learn from evolving cybersecurity environments and adapt detection
strategies by updating rules and decision policies based on new threat patterns and
feedback [14].
6.SecurityandGovernanceChallenges:Investigatechallengesindeployingagentic
AI for cybersecurity, including scalability, safety, transparency, ethical governance,
and resilience against adversarial manipulation of AI models [8], [9].

1.7.3 Emerging Models and Hybrid Frameworks
Federated learning enables cross-institution fraud defense without sharing raw data. This
is really helpful because it adds a level of explainability and control. It also makes
sure that the system is fair and trustworthy. Federated learning is used with gover-
nance(AIGA/SAGA) , which is like a set of rules that makes sure the system is working
correctly. This is important for things like finance, where people need to be able to trust
the system.[3][6]
Federated learning is also used with something called reinforcement learning which’s
a type of artificial intelligence. This type of learning helps the system respond quickly to
changes. It also raises some concerns about safety and stability. To make sure the system
is working correctly people use something called vectorized feature stores, which’s like a
big database that stores all the information. They also use something called agent-to-agent
coordination, which’s like a way for different parts of the system to talk to each other.[3][5]
One of the challenges of using reinforcement learning in real-world situations is that
it needs to be controlled. This is because the system is making decisions on its own and
people need to make sure it is not doing anything wrong. To control the system people
use something called guardrails, which’s like a set of rules that the system has to follow.
They also use something called sim-to-real certification, which’s like a test to make sure
the system is working correctly in the real world.[6]
Agentic AI-Driven Hybrid Detection Models:Modern frameworks integrate tra-
ditional anomaly detection algorithms withagentic AI systemsto detect and respond
to cyber threats. These architectures enable intelligent agents to analyze system behavior
and automatically respond to cyber threats, improving real-time security monitoring [7],
[13].
Multi-AgentCybersecurityFrameworks:Systemsusemultipleintelligentagents
that work together to detect anomalies and respond to threats. These distributed ar-

environments [11].
Sequence-Aware and Behavioral Anomaly Detection:Hybrid models combine
anomaly detection algorithms withsequence-aware analysisto detect abnormal behav-
ioral patterns and suspicious system interactions in real time [12].

Limitations of Existing Datasets:
Despite the availability of benchmark datasets, several challenges remain in cybersecu-
rity dataset research. Many datasets containoutdated attack patterns, lack diversity
in network configurations, and suffer fromclass imbalance, where normal traffic signifi-
cantly outnumbers malicious activity. Additionally, most datasets focus on specific attack
scenarios and may not fully represent the complexity of real-world cybersecurity environ-
ments.
Agentic AI-based systems aim to overcome these limitations by enablingadaptive
learning and real-time anomaly detection, allowing cybersecurity systems to contin-
uously learn from dynamic environments rather than relying solely on static datasets [7],
[13].

2.1 Introduction to the Foundations Chapter
The evolving landscape informs this research on cybersecurity in the context of advances in
Machine Learning and Artificial Intelligence. Traditional security mechanisms that make
use of Artificial Intelligence systems have found it difficult to adapt to the changing en-
vironment due to the presence of fixed rule sets and high dependency on human input
[3], [4]. Thus, such security measures have proven to be insufficient in handling the ever-
changing threat landscape. Considering the emergence of advanced forms of cyber-attacks
using Artificial Intelligence systems, there arises an urgent requirement for self-regulating
mechanisms.
Agentic Artificial Intelligence marks the evolution of this field, which is different from
the concept of Artificial Intelligence. Agentic Artificial Intelligence is capable of providing
long-term memory, planning, and tool-integration, which enables these tools to effectively
operate in complex environments and respond to security threats in real time without
the requirement for human intervention "in the loop" [1], [2]. Within this chapter, the
algorithmic, theoretical, and architectural building blocks to design the entire security
framework using Agentic Artificial Intelligence will be established.
2.2 Theoretical Underpinnings and Conceptual Framework
The present research employs a group of technical terms to describe the autonomous se-
curity framework. Agentic AI is described in this research as "a system in which cognition
and execution can occur independently in uncertain environments" [1]. Moreover, this can
be further supported by "User and Entity Behavior Analytics" (UEBA), which emphasizes
"detecting anomalies by establishing baselines for human and machine entity behaviors"
[3]. Finally, the implementation of defensive actions can be carried out through "SOAR"
(Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response), which replaces human interventions
through "high-speed workflows" [3].
From the perspective of data analysis, "anomaly" can be defined as "a significant de-
viation from a system’s predicted behavior," which necessitates the use of "multimodal
data" that combines different data streams such as "sensor data" and "network data" [15].
Moreover, the logic behind the agent’s evolution can be supported by "reinforcement learn-
ing" (RL). In RL, "agents learn to transition between different states by executing actions
according to policies that optimize reward signals from the environment" [6].
Agentic AI refers to intelligent systems capable of perception, reasoning, planning,
and action execution in dynamic environments. Unlike traditional AI models, which are
reactive, agentic systems are proactive and goal-oriented.

2.4 Overview of Problem-Solving Paradigms
Throughout history, the identification of cyber threats has followed the evolution of several
technologically advanced stages. Early frameworks were designed using signature-based
and rule-based logic. Although these frameworks ensured high transparency and process-
ing speeds, they were unable to address the flexibility required to effectively counter the
evolving exploits. Later, the application of machine learning technology ensured the adop-
tion of a more analytical and data-centric approach. Even this technology was unable to
overcome the challenges associated with the high dependency on manual feature selection
and the inability to predict unknown zero-day attacks [9].
Anothermajorshiftintheevolutionofthefieldcamewiththeadoptionofdeeplearning
techniques. These techniques ensured the application of LSTMs, Transformers, and CNNs
to automatically extract complex features from the huge streams of data. Today, the field is
moving towards the application of agent-based AI, which is characterized by the application
of entities. Unlike earlier approaches, this paradigm enables the application of constant
surveillance and incident response, transforming the field from reactive to self-governing
and proactive [7], [13].
Agentic AI represents an advanced paradigm where systems not only detect anomalies
but also reason and take autonomous actions. This paradigm shifts the focus from reactive

defense to proactive and adaptive cybersecurity systems capable of operating in dynamic
environments.
2.5 Algorithmic Foundations
The computational framework of this research includes sequence modeling, anomaly detec-
tion, and decision optimization. Classification and clustering are the primary methods for
flagging behavioral anomalies in the system [3]. However, to cater to the time-constrained
nature of cybersecurity data, LSTM and Transformer models are also being used. The for-
mer is particularly good at extracting temporal features, which are essential for detecting
complex and non-linear forms of attacks [3].
Optimization of responses is also being carried out by implementing reinforcement
learning. This allows autonomous agents to respond to changing adversarial behaviors by
adapting their strategies [6]. Finally, data sovereignty is being addressed by implementing
federatedlearning. Thisallowsmultipleorganizationstotrainmodelsandshareintelligence
without compromising data privacy and sovereignty [3].
2.6 Classical Algorithms
Typically, a traditional cybersecurity framework incorporates existing machine learning
algorithms such as logistic regression, support vector machines, and decision trees. Such
traditional models offer a high level of transparency and computational efficiency. There-
fore, these traditional models serve as powerful tools for identifying known security risks
[3].
However, despite the significant benefits of traditional machine learning models, these
models have a fixed architecture that does not take into account the non-linear complexities
of modern network data. The traditional models’ use of human-based feature selection
makes them inflexible tools for identifying new zero-day risks. Therefore, these traditional
models are not sufficiently effective in identifying complex and evolving patterns of modern
cyber threats [9].
2.7 Modern Algorithmic Extensions
Modern security strategies are a step forward from traditional strategies, incorporating
deeplearningandautonomoustechnologies. Byemployingdeepensemblingtechniquesthat
combine CNN, LSTM, and Transformer models, these systems can achieve high accuracy.
This is due to their capability to effectively handle both spatial and temporal features
hidden in complex data flows [3].
The incorporation of agentic orchestration has taken a significant step toward advanc-
ing cybersecurity processes, introducing new dimensions of reasoning, planning, and tool-
interaction capabilities. This takes a step closer to achieving autonomous decision-making
processes in cybersecurity [1], [2]. To keep these systems at their peak, reinforcement learn-
ing techniques are employed to adapt defensive strategies based on environmental feedback
[6]. The current state-of-the-art strategy in this domain is represented by hybrid mod-
els, which are complex frameworks incorporating anomaly-based and predictive analytics,
along with agent-based reasoning capabilities [7], [13].

2.11 Data Preprocessing and Representation
The first part of this framework is concerned with the meticulous cleaning, normalization,
and transformation of raw cybersecurity telemetry. This is an essential part of the frame-
work, especially when considering the need to translate raw inputs into the system, such as
raw logs. Through the use of automated log parsing and feature extraction, the framework
is successful in translating raw logs into structured datasets that are necessary for machine
learning analysis [15].
In order to further improve the analytical capabilities of the framework, complex data
representation techniques are used. These techniques include sequence encoding and fea-
ture stores. These techniques are more than just mere data organization techniques. They
are techniques that enable the framework to map out complex relationships and under-
stand the essential time dependencies that are necessary in cybersecurity. Through this
representation, the framework is able to identify even complex time-delay attacks [3].
2.12 Core Processing Unit
The brain of this framework, so to speak, is the core processing unit, a central core through
which anomaly detection models, agentic reasoning models, and orchestration models con-
verge. This framework, therefore, uses deep learning models to detect anomalies from the
norm, while the agentic models assess the environment to determine what course of ac-
tion would be most appropriate. To make this framework scalable and able to perform
distributed oversight through a growing network, a multi-agent architecture has been in-
corporated, allowing for both scalability and parallel processing ability [11], [13].
2.13 Output Generation Layer
The last layer, namely the Output Generation Layer, has the responsibility of converting
the result obtained from the core unit into a form that can be implemented, such as risk
evaluation, security alerts, and even defensive actions. This layer has a direct interface with
Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response tools, ensuring that actions are imple-
mented in a prompt manner without requiring human intervention. However, the system
does not stop at just implementing actions; rather, it also seeks to ensure transparency
and accountability through a complete reasoning trail and decision logs, ensuring that all
actions implemented by the system are "explainable" and can be traced by security teams
to understand the logic behind a particular mitigation strategy [3].

without compromising efficiency, complex feature selection methods are implemented. This
process filters out the noise and the most impactful data points, thus directly increasing
the efficiency of the model.
Although the process of manual engineering is a structured approach, the inclusion of

2.15 Experimental Setup and Design Considerations
The research architecture of this study is based on a robust validation approach, which
begins with the systematic splitting of the available datasets into separate training sets,
validation sets, and testing sets. To guarantee the robustness and reliability of the devel-
oped models, the approach uses cross-validation methods. This is important in reducing the
risk of overfitting while maintaining the consistency of the system’s prediction capabilities
with different and unseen data profiles [14].
Besides data splitting, there are some essential design aspects incorporated to ensure
data integrity. These aspects include tackling data imbalance issues, which is a major
problem for cybersecurity datasets. It is followed by data quality and experiment repro-
ducibility. In order to bridge the gap between static analysis and practical application, this
study utilizes simulation environments. These environments allow for the safe exploration
of autonomous agent behaviors, giving a chance to test how agents reason and respond to
complex threats before practical application [1], [2].
2.16 Evaluation Metrics and Justification
In order to fully understand the efficacy of the proposed system, a framework has been
developed and categorized into three different categories, namely detection, safety, and
reliability. The detection metrics are considered to be the main criteria for measuring the
efficacy of the system in identifying anomalies effectively. This includes standard metrics
such as precision, recall, F1 score, and Area Under Precision Recall Curve, which enable
a fine-grained evaluation of the accuracy of the system in diagnosing anomalies effectively
[3].
In addition to detection, safety metrics are also incorporated in the framework to assess
how effectively the system performs under pressure. Metrics such as policy compliance, risk
ratio, and attack success rate are critical in measuring how effectively the system performs
under pressure and how effectively autonomous actions are carried out within authorized
boundaries [2], [5]. To make the framework more viable, reliability metrics are incorporated
toassesshoweffectivelythesystemperformsunderrepeatedtrials, ensuringthatitsefficacy
does not reduce over time or under different environmental conditions [1].

rather a fundamental requirement in order to gain human trust in automated decision-
making. One major challenge facing contemporary cybersecurity has been that many
state-of-the-art deep learning architectures tend to be "black boxes," where the underlying
reasoning behind a particular threat detection mechanism remains obscure and hard to
interpret by security analysts [13]. In order to address this challenge, agentic systems have
been designed with a layered approach to transparency. By providing detailed reasoning
traces, extensive action logs, and strong auditing capabilities, agentic systems offer a "paper
trail" that explains every autonomous decision taken by the system.
In order to further enhance human-in-the-loop decision-making in critical domains,
the framework has been extended with a suite of Explainable AI (XAI) techniques. These
techniques will allow security analysts to interpret complex neural network patterns, thus
demystifying the underlying reasoning behind a particular threat detection mechanism. By
providing human analysts with detailed reasoning traces, security analysts will be able to
verify whether autonomous decision-making has been justifiable and transparent, rather
than simply expedient.
2.19 Implementation Toolchains (Conceptual View)
The practical implementation of the proposed system is grounded in an already well-
developed Python-based system, utilizing well-known and well-supported deep learning
frameworks such as PyTorch and TensorFlow. However, to take the system beyond the
realm of static detection, the system is designed to integrate well-known and well-supported
multi-agent frameworks, designed to enable true agentic capabilities. The frameworks are
then augmented by the implementation of Large Language Models (LLMs), acting as the
cognitive core, responsible for the high-level reasoning and decision-making necessary to
effectively navigate the complex world of security [15].
To handle the high levels of computational power required by modern cybersecurity, the
system is designed to take advantage of cloud computing and distributed system technolo-
gies. The infrastructure is designed to ensure the horizontal scalability necessary to handle
the high levels of data throughput and analysis without sacrificing system performance [9].
Supporting the infrastructure is an array of automated data preprocessing and monitoring
tools, designed to handle the end-to-end lifecycle of data management. By streamlining
everything from the initial data ingestion to the end-state performance monitoring, the
implementation is designed to ensure that the agentic logic is supported by clean, relevant,
and timely data.

2.21 Ethical, Legal, and Societal Implications (ELSI)
The incorporation of Agentic AI in the domain of cybersecurity has given rise to a complex
gamut of ethical and legal dilemmas, primarily related to data privacy, algorithmic bias,
and the underlying principle of accountability itself [8]. This is due to the fact that, owing
to its autonomous nature, Agentic AI cannot operate in a vacuum and hence has to be
aligned with existing regulatory frameworks, ensuring not only its efficacy but also its
safety and socially acceptable nature.
In order to avert any potential risks associated with Agentic AI, a high degree of
governance frameworks has to be incorporated, ensuring that there is no scope for its
potential misuse and a high degree of transparency, especially in critical industries such as
finance [5]. This can be ensured through a proper framework of protocols, ensuring a fine
line between the rapid response nature of Agentic AI and its underlying human-defined
nature.
2.22 Challenges and Limitations in Theory or Practice
The move towards autonomous security is also faced with a number of challenges, such
as those concerning datasets, scalability, and generalization. The major problem is that

a number of datasets, especially those used for cybersecurity, are no longer valid, as they
lack the modern diversity required to effectively train AI models for modern, real-world
scenarios. This "data aging" is a major problem, as it limits the potential for AI systems
to effectively respond to modern, high-sophistication cyber-attacks [14].
In addition to these challenges, the use of agentic systems is also faced with a number of
operational risks, such as the potential for adversarial manipulation, where an attacker may
use the AI logic to their own advantage, and the potential for "unsafe" actions, especially
in a crisis situation. Finally, the complexity that arises when the number of autonomous
systems increases is a major problem, often resulting in synchronization conflicts and poor
resource allocation [1], [2], [5].
2.23 Lessons from Iterative Development and Peer Feedback
The path to an effective high-performance cybersecurity system is described in terms of an
iterative development process and an evaluation cycle. While this is not meant to be taken
literally, it is more of a figure of speech to describe the continuous improvement process
based on the outputs of rigorous experiments and standardized benchmarking. Through
the analysis of the performance outputs, specific methodological limitations are also iden-
tifiable, thus allowing for improvements to be made to the efficacy of the cybersecurity
system [1].
In addition to the internal improvement process of the cybersecurity system, the in-
fluence of the scientific community on the development of cybersecurity systems is also
significant. Peer-reviewed research is essential to provide the necessary validation for novel
approaches to cybersecurity. Through the scrutiny of novel methodologies, cybersecurity
systems are developed on the foundation of reliability and empirical evidence. This shared
knowledge does not just contribute to the improvement of individual cybersecurity systems
but also to the improvement of the entire industry to develop more robust, transparent,
and trustworthy AI-driven cybersecurity solutions.
2.24 Responsible Use of Generative AI in Foundations Work
The introduction of generative AI in the cybersecurity world also comes with a tremendous
responsibility to adhere to ethical standards and academic integrity. To this end, and
in order to fully comply with all relevant regulations, there has to be a commitment to
transparency and proper citation of all foundational work in its implementation and use [8].
This serves as a foundation for a professional and credible approach to security research,
especially in a world in which automated content generation can so easily be used to mask
true origins and intent.
Other than these ethical considerations, however, the philosophy behind the operation
and use of this system revolves around the concept of human augmentation. This means,
rather than seeing generative AI as a replacement for human intervention, we believe
that its most effective use comes in the form of a sophisticated aid to human decision-
making, especially in a security world in which nuanced human judgment, intuition, and
accountability are absolutely critical and cannot be replaced by a machine, no matter how
advanced.
